\begin{abstract}
Thermal cameras make nighttime pedestrian segmentation reliable, but cost and bandwidth
keep RGB-Thermal (RGB-T) perception out of low-cost platforms. We ask how many thermal
pixels are needed and where they should be used. On MFNet, the thermal teacher's
nighttime pedestrian advantage cannot be transported to an RGB-only student by logit
distillation, modality dropout, or thermal hallucination (night-person TCE $\approx
-0.08$): the evidence is modality-borne. Sweeping the thermal sensor from $60\times80$ to
$240\times320$ reveals a knee at $120\times160$: $1/16$ of RGB pixels recovers 84\% of
the full-resolution RGB-T teacher's nighttime pedestrian gain. Used uniformly,
low-resolution thermal is still no substitute for RGB: at this budget it reaches 41.67
overall mIoU, 14.7 points below RGB, although its nighttime pedestrian IoU is 17.2 points
higher. We introduce a person-aware reliability gate: a small U-Net, trained on a
held-out training split with hard-condition augmentation, decides per pixel when to defer
from an RGB expert to the $120\times160$ thermal expert, and a structural constraint
restricts thermal overrides to pedestrians. With three seeds and a frozen 392-image test,
the gate raises overall mIoU from 56.38 to $57.48\pm0.11$ and nighttime pedestrian IoU
from 45.24 to $62.26\pm1.02$; all other classes change by at most 0.08. Removing the
person constraint reaches 57.74 but moves car-stop and guardrail IoU by $-1.96$ and
$+1.60$. Under synthetic low-light and noise, RGB collapses to 47.79; the constrained gate
holds 49.34, the unconstrained gate 51.27. Thermal sensing should be budgeted and
class-routed, not distilled or uniformly deployed.
\end{abstract}
