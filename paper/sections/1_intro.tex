\section{Introduction}

Thermal infrared cameras are the standard remedy for the failure of visible-spectrum
perception at night. In RGB-Thermal (RGB-T) semantic segmentation, a thermal stream
recovers pedestrians, cyclists, and unlit obstacles that RGB misses, and a decade of
fusion architectures has turned this observation into accurate daytime and nighttime
scene parsing \cite{ha2017mfnet,sun2019rtfnet,zhang2022crossmodal,zhang2023delivering}.
The recipe, however, assumes a thermal sensor that is neither cheap nor plentiful.
Long-wave infrared arrays are coarser and more expensive than their RGB counterparts,
and the bandwidth and storage of a full thermal stream are difficult to justify on a
low-cost robot, a delivery vehicle, or a wearable \cite{shivakumar2019rgbthermal}.
The community has responded by treating thermal as either a full-resolution input or
as something to be distilled away. Both responses sidestep the budget question: how
many thermal pixels are actually needed, and which pixels should carry thermal
evidence?

This paper answers that question empirically on MFNet \cite{ha2017mfnet}, the standard
day/night RGB-T benchmark. We first test whether the thermal advantage can be moved
into an RGB-only student. Logit distillation, modality dropout, and thermal
hallucination all fail: the nighttime pedestrian gain of a full-resolution thermal
teacher does not survive the transfer, and the night-person teacher-consistency
efficiency (TCE) is approximately $-0.08$. The evidence is not a representation that
RGB can learn to imitate; it is carried by the thermal photons themselves. This
negative result motivates a sensor-budget study rather than another distillation
objective.

We then sweep the thermal stream from $60\times80$ to $240\times320$ while holding the
RGB stream, backbone, schedule, and evaluation protocol fixed. The resulting curve has
a clear knee at $120\times160$, one sixteenth of the RGB pixel count: this budget
recovers 81\% of the full-resolution teacher's nighttime pedestrian gain at a cost of
only 0.58 overall mIoU relative to a 120-epoch RGB-only model. Pushing to $60\times80$
loses 3.4 points of nighttime pedestrian IoU for a negligible further saving, while
$240\times320$ buys only 0.6 additional points. The knee is therefore a useful
operating point, not an arbitrary compromise.

A low-resolution thermal stream cannot simply be fused globally. At $120\times160$ the
thermal expert is better than RGB on pedestrians but worse on cars, curbs, guardrails,
and road markings; a globally fused model inherits both effects and ends at 55.80
overall mIoU, below the RGB-only 56.38. A per-class gate selected on training accuracy
does not repair this: the training split is much easier than the official test split,
and the selector collapses to the thermal expert on every class. The failure is
instructive. The routing decision is spatial and condition-dependent, and it must be
selected on data that resembles the deployment distribution, not on clean training
accuracy.

We introduce a person-aware reliability gate that makes the budget usable. Two frozen
experts---a 120-epoch RGB model and a thermal-only model on the $120\times160$
stream---propose dense labels. A small U-Net reads the RGB image, the thermal image,
both probability maps, and their confidence and entropy, and predicts a per-pixel
reliability logit for the thermal expert. At deployment, the gate may override RGB only
where the experts disagree and either expert predicts a pedestrian. The constraint is
structural: pedestrians are the safety-critical class, and the eight other classes
should keep RGB's higher-resolution evidence. The gate is trained on a 627-image
subset of the training split with hard-condition augmentation, selected on the
remaining 157 images, and the official 392-image test is touched once.

With three gate-training seeds, the gate reaches $57.57\pm0.07$ overall mIoU and
$61.36\pm0.32$ nighttime pedestrian IoU, against 56.38 and 45.24 for RGB-only and
55.80 and 61.88 for the thermal expert alone. The entire gain is the pedestrian class:
all other classes change by at most 0.37 IoU. Under synthetic low-light, blur, and
noise, RGB collapses from 56.38 to 47.95 overall, while the gate holds 51.10 and the
thermal expert holds 62.02 nighttime pedestrian IoU. A compute measurement closes the
accounting: the two-expert system costs 42.6 ms per $480\times640$ frame on an RTX
4090, 2.6 times the RGB-only 11.0 ms. The contribution is a sensor-pixel budget, not a
compute budget, and we report the difference rather than hiding it.

Our contributions are: (i) a negative diagnosis showing that the nighttime pedestrian
advantage is modality-borne and does not distil into RGB; (ii) a thermal sensor-budget
curve with a $120\times160$ knee and a per-class explanation of why global
low-resolution fusion is insufficient; (iii) a person-aware reliability gate with a
train-internal selection protocol and three-seed evidence of safety-at-parity; and
(iv) a degradation study showing when the gate and the thermal expert remain useful
after RGB is corrupted. We do not claim state-of-the-art overall mIoU, real-sensor
validation, single-model compute parity, or generalization beyond MFNet; those are
explicit limitations, and we return to them in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
